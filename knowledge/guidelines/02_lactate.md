# Lactate in Suspected Sepsis
Source: Evans L. et al., Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines, Crit Care Med 2021; Singer M. et al., Sepsis-3, JAMA 2016
Year: 2021
URL: https://www.sccm.org/survivingsepsiscampaign/guidelines-and-resources/surviving-sepsis-campaign-adult-guidelines-2021

## Interpreting raised lactate
Serum lactate is a marker of tissue hypoperfusion and cellular stress, but it is not a direct measure of perfusion. Raised lactate (hyperlactataemia, above 2 mmol/L) in a patient with suspected infection is associated with higher mortality. Values of 4 mmol/L or more indicate a high-risk state and, together with hypotension, should prompt urgent resuscitation. Lactate can also rise from adrenergic stimulation, liver dysfunction, seizures, certain drugs and impaired clearance, so it must be interpreted with the clinical picture.

## Measuring lactate
Lactate should be measured early in adults with suspected sepsis. Venous and arterial samples are both acceptable for initial assessment; a raised venous value can be confirmed arterially if needed. Lactate should be remeasured within two to four hours when the initial value is raised, because the trend is more informative than a single value.

## Lactate-guided resuscitation
For adults with sepsis or septic shock, guiding resuscitation to decrease serum lactate is suggested over not using lactate. A falling lactate suggests improving perfusion, while a persistently high or rising lactate should prompt reassessment of volume status, cardiac function, vasopressor need and source control. Normalisation of lactate is a goal of resuscitation but should not be pursued with unlimited fluid.

## Capillary refill time
Capillary refill time can complement lactate as a bedside marker of perfusion. The 2021 guidance suggests using capillary refill time to guide resuscitation as an adjunct to other measures of perfusion. It is quick, repeatable and responds faster than lactate to changes in perfusion.

## Lactate in septic shock criteria
Under Sepsis-3, septic shock requires both a vasopressor requirement to keep mean arterial pressure at or above 65 mmHg and a serum lactate above 2 mmol/L after adequate fluid resuscitation. The combination of hypotension and raised lactate carries a substantially higher mortality than either alone.
